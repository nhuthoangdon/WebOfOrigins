<h2>Understanding Glucosamine</h2>
<p>Glucosamine is one of the most abundant amino sugars (modified monosaccharides) found in living organisms. In humans, it is an important compound involved in building and maintaining joints and cartilage. However, unlike other bioactive compounds such as certain vitamins or fatty acids, glucosamine is not a nutrient that needs to come directly from food. The human body makes its own glucosamine by converting blood glucose (a simple sugar) and glutamine (an amino acid made by the body or obtained from protein-rich foods) through a series of chemical reactions1. The resulting glucosamine is then used to make larger molecules that form important parts of cartilage, connective tissues, and synovial fluid.

Glucosamine is not unique to humans, it serves as an essential structural building block across diverse living organisms. In vertebrates, it is found most abundantly in connective tissues such as cartilage, tendons, and joint fluid; in arthropods, it is used to make chitin, a main component of their exoskeletons; in fungi, it is a major component of their rigid cell walls; and in bacteria, it is a vital part of the polymers that make their cell walls while also serving as a nutrient source2. Chitin is the structure that contains the highest proportion of glucosamine as it is made entirely of repeating chains of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc, a modified form of glucosamine). 


Glucosamine production sources & impacts

Commercial glucosamine is usually produced by breaking down chitin sourced from crustacean processing byproducts or fungi, or through direct microbial fermentation using specialized strains of bacteria.

Crustacean Shells
Commercial glucosamine is predominantly sourced from chitin extracted from discarded shells of crustaceans, especially shrimp, crab, and lobster generated by the large seafood industry. Shrimp and crab have been the dominant commercial chitin and chitosan feedstocks worldwide due to the popularity and availability of these seafood species, while lobster shells contribute to a smaller, niche research-focused sector3.

Impacts: 
The global shellfish industry generates over 12-14 million tonnes of waste annually4, creating a potentially abundant supply of raw material for chitin production. In this sense, chitin manufacturing offers a way to turn part of this waste into a useful resource while helping address the seafood waste problem. However, in reality, only a tiny fraction of the discarded shells is currently utilised, including for chitin extraction. Much of the remainder ends up in landfills or is discharged into the sea, largely because of the costs and logistical challenges involved in collecting, storing, and processing it. As a result, much of the environmental burden remains unresolved, even as global demand for chitin and its derivatives remains strong and continues to grow5. Furthermore, chitin production itself requires strong acids and bases and generates a lot of wastewater, adding another environmental cost to the process.

(i) Antarctic krill processing byproducts from the pigment (astaxanthin) and krill oil industries can also be utilised for chitin extraction. These byproducts offer a high-yield, clean, consistent, and relatively low-mineral raw material. However, Antarctic krill is a highly contentious source from an environmental perspective, with krill fisheries facing concerns related to overfishing, illegal and unregulated fishing, and climate change. Antarctic krill are a crucial component of Antarctic ecosystems and serve as a major food source for many marine predators, making them an important link in the Antarctic food web. Although regulatory management measures are in place, compliance and catch limits are not always guaranteed due to several factors6. As of 2025, Norway, China, South Korea, Chile, and Ukraine accounted for 100% of the reported Antarctic krill catch. Since the 2000s, annual Antarctic krill catches have shown a continuing upward trend7.

Fungi
Glucosamine can also be made from chitin extracted from the cell walls of filamentous fungi, which has the same chemical structure as chitin from shellfish. Fungal chitin offers a more environmentally friendly, vegan-friendly, and allergy-free source of glucosamine. The production process involves fungal fermentation, in which manufacturers feed a specific fungal strain, most commonly Aspergillus niger, a nutrient-rich, plant-based sugar broth typically derived from non-GMO corn or wheat. Once the fungi have grown into a dense, chitin-rich biomass, it is harvested and processed to extract and purify the chitin. The chitin can then undergo further processing, such as hydrolysis and crystallisation, to produce derivatives such as glucosamine or chitosan.

Impacts: While fungi-derived glucosamine does not require the same harsh chemical treatment of raw materials as shellfish-derived glucosamine, uses much less water, and generates less waste, it comes with its own drawbacks. Its production relies on fresh, plant-based raw materials such as corn or wheat, along with additional processing to produce the clean sugar nutrients needed to feed the fungi. Cultivating these crops comes with the environmental impacts of agriculture, including monoculture farming, arable land use, water consumption, and the use of fertilisers and pesticides.

Corn-derived glucosamine: direct microbial fermentation
There is a newer alternative method that bypasses the chitin intermediate by directly fermenting glucosamine from plant-derived glucose, particularly corn, using specialised engineered bacteria such as E. coli or yeast strains. These microorganisms convert glucose and nitrogen from the nutrient medium into glucosamine, which then undergoes downstream stabilisation to produce a mineral salt form such as glucosamine HCl or glucosamine sulfate. Other starchy plants such as wheat or soy may also be used instead of corn, providing an alternative for the smaller niche market of consumers with corn allergies. By eliminating the chitin extraction step, this method can use fewer resources and generate less waste than fungi-derived glucosamine production, while offering the same vegan-friendly, environmentally conscious, and allergy-free benefits.

Impacts:
Because corn or other starchy plants are used as the primary raw material, this method shares some of the environmental impacts of fungi-derived glucosamine, particularly those associated with crop agriculture. These include monoculture farming, arable land use, high water consumption, and the use of fertilisers and pesticides.

Alternative sources with limited production

Microalgae
Chitin may also be extracted from microalgae, offering a potential green, low-impact source that requires less use of harsh chemicals, land, and water while generating less wastewater8. Despite this potential, algae-derived chitin is rarely used for commercial production due to low yields, expensive equipment, and the specialised technology required for its extraction. Nevertheless, algae are already used as a sustainable source of other high-value compounds, such as omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA), astaxanthin (a red-orange pigment with strong antioxidant properties), and other valuable pigments and proteins.

Insects
As environmental concerns rise, insects are being studied not only as an alternative protein source but also as a source of chitin. Their exoskeletons contain high levels of chitin, similar to those of crustaceans, while insect farming can require less land and water and may generate less waste. However, insect-derived chitin is currently used mainly for industrial and agricultural applications rather than human consumption, partly due to regulatory considerations and consumer acceptance compared with crustacean- or plant-derived sources.

You might want to know
(i) Currently, crustacean-derived glucosamine dominates the market as a popular and affordable source, while corn-derived glucosamine occupies a more specialised market as a vegan, eco-friendly, and shellfish-free alternative.

(i) Glucosamine is commonly sold as glucosamine HCl because it contains a higher proportion of pure glucosamine by weight than glucosamine sulfate. This allows more glucosamine to be provided in the same capsule or tablet size. Glucosamine HCl can also be cheaper to produce because it does not require the additional processing needed to convert glucosamine into the stabilised sulfate form.

Verdicts

Most, if not all, glucosamine sources come with existing or potential environmental impacts.

Crustacean-derived glucosamine offers a cheaper product while making use of seafood processing waste generated by the massive fisheries and aquaculture industries. Its caveat is the processing waste generated during the treatment of raw materials and chitin extraction.

Fungi-derived and direct microbial fermentation glucosamine offer modern alternatives with less waste and no shellfish allergen. However, they do not make use of the abundant seafood waste generated by the fisheries and aquaculture industries, while also relying on plant materials whose cultivation can bring similar environmental impacts.

Other alternative sources are currently not viable for large-scale dedicated glucosamine production due to limitations in scale, economics, and regulatory considerations.

Environmentally conscious consumers should opt for products with traceable and transparent ingredients. There will always be trade-offs in manufacturing and large-scale production, but these impacts can be reduced by prioritising more sustainable raw materials. For instance, crustacean-derived glucosamine can be sourced from seafood produced through sustainable fisheries and aquaculture practices, while corn- or other plant-derived glucosamine can be produced using sustainably grown crops.

Worth knowing: Global fisheries and aquaculture production was projected to reach 198 million tonnes in 20259, with continued growth potential. This means the waste burden is unlikely to cease unless better solutions are developed or seafood consumption declines. With the world’s population projected to continue growing for at least another six decades10, seafood consumption is unlikely to decline significantly, potentially increasing the waste burden and resource consumption in the coming years. This calls for less aggressive and more rigorous waste and byproduct processing, alongside better resource management strategies.
 
Chitin and its derivatives, such as chitosan, are gaining popularity for a wide range of applications, including agriculture (biostimulants, biopesticides), wastewater treatment (removing toxins and contaminants), medicine (bandages, wound dressings, tissue engineering), food and dietary products (food coatings, skincare, fat binders), and packaging (biodegradable packaging, biofilms, gelling agents). So far, demand for chitin remains consistently high, while barriers to processing raw materials prevent shell waste from being fully utilised, leaving a gap between supply and demand11. Solving these supply chain issues is a highly complex matter that requires global efforts. Finding alternative sources for chitin does not directly reduce seafood waste, while better extraction and processing technologies, together with local management at processing sites, might address the problem more directly.
