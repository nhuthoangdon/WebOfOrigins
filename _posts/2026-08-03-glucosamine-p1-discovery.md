Glucosamine is a naturally occurring amino sugar found across many living organisms, including plants, fungi, bacteria, and animals. In the human body, it is present in the highest concentrations in articular cartilage (the tissue covering joint surfaces), intervertebral discs (the cartilaginous cushions between the vertebrae of the spine), and synovial fluid (the lubricating fluid within movable joints). In these tissues, glucosamine serves as a precursor to glycosaminoglycans, long-chain complex sugars that combine with proteins to form proteoglycans - key components of the extracellular matrix that provide structure, resilience, hydration, and shock-absorbing properties to connective tissues such as cartilage and joints. Because of this role, glucosamine has become a popular ingredient in joint supplements, although clinical evidence for its effectiveness when consumed orally remains mixed.

<h2>The Story of Glucosamine</h2>

<h3>1876</h3>
<p>Glucosamine was first discovered by German orthopedic surgeon and scientist Georg Ledderhose, who isolated it by treating his leftover lobster shells with hydrochloric acid, yielding glucosamine hydrochloride (glucosamine HCl) - a solid, crystalline salt composed of glucosamine and chloride ions. He named the compound “glycosamine”.</p>

<span><b>Did you know? </b>Georg Ledderhose is also the namesake of Ledderhose disease <i>(plantar fibromatosis)</i>, a rare but benign condition he first documented in 1894. It is characterised by slow-growing, firm nodules that develop on the sole of the foot.</span>

<h3>1898</h3>
<p>A German chemist named Breuer created the first form of glucosamine sulfate by converting glucosamine hydrochloride into a sulfate salt. However, this remained primarily a laboratory experiment and did not advance into practical use at the time, as raw glucosamine sulfate was highly unstable and rapidly degraded when exposed to air and aqueous solutions.</p>

<h3>1902 & 1939</h3>
<h4>1902</h4>
<p>Hermann Leuchs, under the mentorship of Nobel laureate Emil Fischer, achieved the first total synthesis of glucosamine from arabinose - a 5-carbon sugar often found in plants, confirming its structure as an amino sugar. His work produced a pure, controlled sample of the glucosamine molecule and laid the groundwork for further studies in carbohydrate and structural chemistry, contributing to the understanding of amino sugars as fundamental building blocks of biological macromolecules and metabolic pathways.</p>
<h4>1939</h4>
<p>The British chemist Sir Walter Haworth, winner of a Nobel Prize in 1937, mapped the final 3D molecular structure and spatial arrangement (stereochemistry) of the glucosamine molecule that is still used today.</p>

<h3>1930s - 1960s</h3>
<p>This period marked a golden age of modern medicine and biochemistry, shaped by the rise of antibiotics, widespread vaccines, synthetic vitamins, antihistamines, and psychiatric medications, alongside major advances in biochemistry and molecular biology. These advances contributed to the understanding of metabolic pathways and helped elucidate the role of glucosamine in biological processes.</p>

<h3>1960s - 1980s</h3>
<p>As scientists recognised glucosamine’s role in the synthesis of biological macromolecules, they began investigating its potential therapeutic use in improving joint health. During this period, glucosamine sulfate was considered more effective than glucosamine HCl, as it contains glucosamine and sulfate - essential components in glycosaminoglycan synthesis and cartilage structure. However, due to its instability, glucosamine sulfate was not produced commercially or used clinically until the early 1980s, when Italian professor Luigi Rovati and his research group at Rottapharm developed a stabilised crystalline form - a mixed salt of glucosamine sulfate and sodium chloride (NaCl). This co-crystalline structure produced a stable, non-hygroscopic powder, in which the added salt helps stabilise the compound by reducing its exposure to moisture and oxygen. He patented the chemical substance as a prescription drug for the treatment of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.</p>

<span><b>Worth noting: </b>Professor Luigi Rovati was a physician and pharmacologist before leaving academia to establish a research laboratory in Monza, Italy, known as Rotta Research (later becoming Rottapharm). The company grew into a major pharmaceutical firm following the development of stabilised glucosamine sulfate (marketed as Dona). Parts of the business continue to operate today under the Rovati family as Rottapharm Biotech, with a presence in over 85 countries. Rottapharm is historically significant in the pharmaceutical industry, demonstrating that independent R&D can achieve major breakthroughs and scale globally.</span>

<h3>1980s - present</h3>
<p>During the 1980s, glucosamine was discovered to also have anti-inflammatory effects at the cellular level, where it helps slow the enzymes responsible for breaking down joint cartilage. This discovery further increased its therapeutic potential and popularity in the treatment of joint disorders, particularly osteoarthritis. Subsequently, extensive human clinical studies have been conducted since the late 1980s to evaluate its effectiveness.</p>

<h4>Mid 1990s - 2000s</h4>
<p> glucosamine supplements reached peak popularity following the passage of the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) of 1994 by the U.S. Congress, which classified glucosamine as an over-the-counter dietary supplement, as well as the influence of the 1997 best-selling book titled The Arthritis Cure by physician Jason Theodosakis.</p>

<h4>Current Significance</h4>
<p>As of present, glucosamine remains one of the most widely used joint health supplements globally, available in both sulfate and hydrochloride forms and most commonly formulated in combination with chondroitin sulfate. Its popularity continues amid growing demand for joint health products in aging populations. However, clinical evidence regarding its efficacy remains mixed, with conclusions varying across clinical trials, systematic reviews, and recommendations from different medical organisations.</p>
